## 25-disk covering: 6.1 Sol vs 6 Astra — an analysis of the experiments

Sol's performance on this 25-disk covering test surprised me. Before the experiments, I expected neither model to solve the problem on its first attempt: both would run for twelve hours and score zero. Astra/Max's result matched that expectation, but GPT‑6.1 Sol/Max completed the proof in about five hours and scored 230. I wanted to understand what Sol had done right, and why it was so much faster.

My first suspicion was that the difference came mainly from finding reference material. Sol found a public 20-disk covering project early on, containing a complete proof and programs that could provide a starting point for the 25-disk problem. I therefore ran another experiment, putting the references and hints about adapting them directly into Astra's prompt. With no need to find the material itself, I expected Astra might finish in about five hours, or even sooner.

Instead, the second Astra run was interrupted after more than seven hours, still without a complete proof.

### The hard part of the proof

The task asks for the smallest common radius of at most 25 congruent disks covering the entire closed unit disk, a rigorous proof of optimality, and the minimal polynomial of that algebraic radius. The minimal polynomial has degree four. The harder work is proving that the disks cover the target disk and that the radius cannot be smaller.

Sol used adjacency relations between centers to reduce the coverings that needed consideration to finitely many structures. It then ruled out, class by class, the possibilities that might cover the disk with a smaller radius. There are many structures, with infinitely many placements of the centers within each structure, so excluding all these possibilities can require substantial computation. To save time, one needs conditions that are cheap to check but eliminate many possibilities. Once a whole class of structures or a batch of branches is rigorously shown unable to yield a better covering, it can be skipped without calculating each individual case. Finding and using such conditions early can make a large difference to the proof's running time.

### From a 20-disk proof to a 25-disk proof

When I first saw Sol's result, I suspected that its proof might be wrong. After repeatedly checking it against our existing research assets, I confirmed that it was correct. How had Sol managed it?

The experiment records show that Sol located a complete public 20-disk covering proof on GitHub a little over ten minutes into the run. It used the derivations and programs to build a proof framework for 25 disks, then continued studying how to reduce the number of structures that needed exclusion. By about 1 hour 53 minutes and 2 hours 51 minutes, respectively, two conditions that were very cheap to check had been incorporated into the program.

These conditions eliminated many impossible structures. Some branches could be rejected before a complete graph had been generated, saving all the calculations that would otherwise have followed those branches. Sol borrowed from the 20-disk proof, found methods suited to the 25-disk problem, and finished in about five hours, earning 230 points.

### Was Astra just unlucky?

The first Astra run lasted the full twelve hours and scored zero. It produced a candidate covering, local proofs and geometric conclusions, but never completed the global optimality proof. It also did not find and make effective use of an equally efficient set of exclusion conditions.

At that point, I had not concluded that Astra was less capable than Sol. Sol had found the 20-disk proof early, whereas the first Astra run's public execution records did not show it finding or consulting that material. I wondered whether the difference might mainly come from reference retrieval.

I designed a comparison experiment: keep the overall Goal prompt unchanged, give Astra the relevant GitHub links directly, and explicitly ask it to consult that proof before trying again.

### Give Astra the references and try again

I expected that Astra might outperform Sol now that the material was already in front of it. It did consult the references and quickly built a similar proof framework, but it still did not finish the subsequent work.

Sol completed the scored task in less than five hours. Astra did not explicitly state the first exclusion condition until about 5 hours 50 minutes, after which it proved the condition and put it into computation. When the experiment was interrupted at about 7 hours 10 minutes 38 seconds, the global proof remained incomplete. The records also did not show the second condition being fully derived and used for exclusion. The first condition arrived late; the run ended before the second had been fully put to use.

Providing the references and adaptation hints still did not let Astra catch up with Sol. The first run was incomplete after twelve hours; the second was incomplete when stopped after more than seven hours.

### Sol won this round

After the comparison experiment, my initial explanation — perhaps Astra was simply unlucky and had not found the references — was no longer enough. Astra had been given the material, had consulted it, and still had not finished.

What Sol did well in that run was adapt the 20-disk proof to 25 disks, find low-cost exclusion conditions early, and use them in computation. Those conditions saved a great deal of work. Even with the same references available, Astra did not accomplish that step in time. In these 25-disk experiments, Astra clearly lost to Sol. I had given it a second chance, and the result was still the same.

Whether Sol is stronger than Astra across mathematical research as a whole is a question these experiments cannot answer.
